The question

Most of machine learning assumes the data is already there. In medicine and science it rarely is: outcomes arrive late or never, experiments cost weeks, and every answer has a price. My work asks what each piece of information is worth before you pay for it. It comes in five parts.

When a study can recruit from different patient groups and randomize treatments, which experiments should a limited budget buy to find the best treatment policy? The value of an experiment turns out to depend on how likely its outcome is to be observed, relative to its cost, so the experiments worth running under censoring are not the ones an uncensored analysis would pick.
My master's thesis. Patient outcomes are often censored: we only know an event has not happened yet. This paper ranks patient–duration choices by expected information per unit of expected cost, with a (1−1/e) guarantee for the batch, by connecting batch active learning to the classical maximum-coverage problem. Cellular agriculture has very little data and each experiment takes weeks. We compared data-acquisition strategies on real cell-media optimization; Bayesian optimization won.
